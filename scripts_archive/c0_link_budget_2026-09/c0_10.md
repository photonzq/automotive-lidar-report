### 10. Link budget: loss versus range, the detection level, and how range scales

This subsection joins subsections 1, 2 and 7 into one picture. The script `automotive_perception_report_figures/link_budget_scaling.py` draws the three figures below and writes `link_budget_scaling_tables.txt`. All numbers are [E] for a Lambertian target that fills the illuminated spot, at normal incidence, in clear air.

**Variables.**
- $R$: range to the target. $\lambda$: wavelength. $E_{\rm ph}=hc/\lambda$: photon energy ($2.11\times10^{-19}$ J at 940 nm, $1.28\times10^{-19}$ J at 1,550 nm).
- $E_{\rm tx}$: optical energy transmitted per point, summed over all shots of a point (ToF) or over the up–down chirp pair (FMCW).
- $\rho$: Lambertian reflectance, here 0.1. A Lambertian surface that intercepts power $P$ radiates $\rho P/\pi$ per steradian along its normal.
- $D$: diameter of the receive lens (direct detection), or of the shared transmit–receive aperture (monostatic coherent). $A_{\rm rx}=\pi D^2/4$.
- $T_{\rm atm}=e^{-\alpha R}$: one-way atmospheric transmission with extinction coefficient $\alpha$ (subsection 8).
  - In clear air (Kim model, visibility 23 km), $\alpha=0.085$ /km at 940 nm and 0.044 /km at 1,550 nm.
  - $T_{\rm atm}^2$ then costs only 0.15 and 0.08 dB at 200 m.
- $\eta$: end-to-end efficiency from photons at the aperture to signal photoelectrons: optics × detector, and for coherent detection also × mixing. The coherent receiver's mode and polarization factors are kept out of $\eta$ and put into $A_{\rm mode}$ below.
- $n_{\rm req}$: mean signal photoelectrons per point needed for the required detection probability (item (c)).
- Coherent beam parameters:
  - $w_0$: the 1/e² intensity radius of the Gaussian beam at the aperture, its waist for a collimated beam; here $w_0=0.45D$.
  - $w(R)$: the 1/e² radius at the target.
  - $z_R=\pi w_0^2/\lambda$: the Rayleigh range.
  - $t=1-e^{-2(D/2)^2/w_0^2}=1-e^{-2\times1.235}=0.915$: the fraction of the Gaussian's power that passes a hard aperture of diameter $D$. It is called $T$ in §C10.3b.

**(a) Link loss.** The link loss $\mathcal{L}$ is the fraction of the transmitted energy that reaches the receiver in a form it can use, before the detector efficiency. The mean signal per point is then
$$\bar n=\frac{\eta\,E_{\rm tx}}{E_{\rm ph}}\,\mathcal{L}(R),$$
and the link loss in dB is $-10\log_{10}\mathcal{L}$.

*Direct detection.* The lit spot intercepts all the transmitted light and radiates $\rho E_{\rm tx}/\pi$ per steradian toward the sensor. A lens of area $A_{\rm rx}$ subtends $A_{\rm rx}/R^2$ sr, so
$$\mathcal{L}_{\rm DD}(R)=\frac{\rho\,A_{\rm rx}}{\pi R^2}\,T_{\rm atm}^2=\frac{\rho\,D^2}{4R^2}\,T_{\rm atm}^2 .$$
- **Scope.** This holds whatever the spot size, as long as the pixel sees the whole spot. It depends on wavelength only through $T_{\rm atm}$.
- **Worked example:** $D=25$ mm and $R=200$ m give $0.1\times0.025^2/(4\times200^2)=3.9\times10^{-10}$, i.e. 94.1 dB. Clear air adds 0.15 dB, for 94.2 dB.

*Coherent detection: where the mode-equivalent area $A_{\rm mode}$ comes from.* A heterodyne receiver responds only to light in the spatial mode (and polarization) of its local oscillator (LO). Three standard results give its effective collecting area.
1. **Antenna theorem** [I: A. E. Siegman, *Appl. Opt.* 5, 1588 (1966)]. A single-mode receiver has an effective area $A_{\rm eff}(\Omega)$ for light arriving from direction $\Omega$. It obeys $\int A_{\rm eff}\,d\Omega=\lambda^2$ per polarization. This is the $A\Omega\approx\lambda^2$ rule of §C7.
2. **Back-propagated LO** [I: Siegman 1966; B. J. Rye, *Appl. Opt.* 18, 1390 (1979); R. G. Frehlich and M. J. Kavaya, *Appl. Opt.* 30, 5325 (1991)]. The angular pattern of $A_{\rm eff}$ is the LO mode propagated backwards from the detector, through the optics, to the target.
   - Let $\tilde I_B(\mathbf r)$ be that back-propagated irradiance in the target plane, normalized so that $\int\tilde I_B\,d^2r=1$.
   - Light from target point $\mathbf r$ arrives within a solid angle $d\Omega=d^2r/R^2$ and sees $A_{\rm eff}(\mathbf r)=\lambda^2R^2\,\tilde I_B(\mathbf r)$.
   - This is consistent with result 1: $\int A_{\rm eff}\,d\Omega=\lambda^2\int\tilde I_B\,d^2r=\lambda^2$.
3. **Diffuse target: patches add in power.** A rough surface randomizes the optical phase from patch to patch, so on average over speckle their heterodyne powers add.
   - A patch $d^2r$ lit by irradiance $I_T(\mathbf r)$ radiates $(\rho/\pi)\,I_T\,d^2r$ per steradian.
   - The receiver collects this over the area $A_{\rm eff}(\mathbf r)$ at distance $R$, i.e. over $A_{\rm eff}/R^2$ steradians.
   - Summing all patches:
$$P_{\rm het}=\frac{\rho}{\pi}\,\lambda^2\int I_T(\mathbf r)\,\tilde I_B(\mathbf r)\,d^2r .$$

Writing the result in the direct-detection form, $P_{\rm het}=P_{\rm tx}\,\rho A_{\rm mode}/(\pi R^2)$, defines the mode-equivalent collecting area
$$A_{\rm mode}=\lambda^2R^2\int\frac{I_T(\mathbf r)}{P_{\rm tx}}\,\tilde I_B(\mathbf r)\,d^2r .$$
In a monostatic system the transmitted beam and the back-propagated LO are the same Gaussian at the target, $g(r)=\frac{2}{\pi w^2}e^{-2r^2/w^2}$ (normalized to unit power), and $\int g^2\,d^2r=\frac{4}{\pi^2w^4}\cdot\frac{\pi w^2}{4}=\frac{1}{\pi w^2}$. Two further factors complete the result:
- the aperture passes a fraction $t$ of the Gaussian on transmit and again on receive, $t^2$ in all;
- a depolarizing target sends only half its light into the LO's polarization, a factor ½.

$$\boxed{A_{\rm mode}(R)=\frac{t^2\,\lambda^2R^2}{2\pi\,w(R)^2}},\qquad \mathcal{L}_{\rm coh}(R)=\frac{\rho\,A_{\rm mode}}{\pi R^2}\,T_{\rm atm}^2=\frac{\rho\,t^2\lambda^2}{2\pi^2\,w(R)^2}\,T_{\rm atm}^2 .$$

This is the monostatic link equation of Piggott et al. (Waymo) [P], written as an area; the report's scripts use it throughout (§C10.3b).
- **What it assumes:** perfect alignment and mode matching, truncation treated to first order as a power factor on each pass, no atmospheric turbulence (which reduces heterodyne efficiency at long range; Frehlich and Kavaya 1991), and a fully depolarizing Lambertian target.
- **Physical reading.** The single-mode receiver collects the target's radiance times $\lambda^2$. Concentrating the light on a smaller spot raises the radiance, and with it the signal.

*A collimated beam* (waist at the aperture) spreads as $w(R)^2=w_0^2\,[1+(R/z_R)^2]$. $\mathcal{L}_{\rm coh}$ then has three regimes:
- **Near field** ($R\ll z_R$): $w\approx w_0$, so $\mathcal{L}_{\rm coh}\to\rho t^2\lambda^2/(2\pi^2w_0^2)$, *independent of range*. The spot does not grow, so its radiance, and what one mode collects, stays constant. Here a larger aperture is worse ($\propto1/D^2$) and a longer wavelength better ($\propto\lambda^2$).
- **Far field** ($R\gg z_R$): $w\to\lambda R/(\pi w_0)$, so $\mathcal{L}_{\rm coh}\to\rho t^2w_0^2/(2R^2)$. This is the direct-detection $D^2/R^2$ law, reduced by a factor $2t^2(0.45)^2=0.34$ (−4.7 dB).
- **Best collimated aperture at each range.** $w^2=w_0^2+(\lambda R/\pi w_0)^2$ is smallest, at $2\lambda R/\pi$, when $w_0^2=\lambda R/\pi$. Hence
$$\mathcal{L}_{\rm env}(R)=\frac{\rho\,t^2\lambda}{4\pi R}\,T_{\rm atm}^2,\qquad D_{\rm opt}(R)=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda R/\pi}}{0.45}.$$
  - This envelope falls only as $1/R$.
  - At the optimum, $A_{\rm mode}=t^2\lambda R/4=t^2(0.45)^2A_{\rm rx}=0.17\,A_{\rm rx}$.
- **Worked numbers at 1,550 nm, $D=25$ mm:**
  - $w_0=11.25$ mm and $z_R=\pi(0.01125)^2/1.55\times10^{-6}=257$ m.
  - The near-field level is $0.1\times0.838\times(1.55\times10^{-6})^2/(2\pi^2\times0.01125^2)=8.1\times10^{-11}$, i.e. 100.9 dB.
  - At 200 m, $w=11.25\sqrt{1+(200/257)^2}=14.3$ mm, so $A_{\rm mode}=63$ mm² and $\mathcal{L}_{\rm coh}=0.1\times6.3\times10^{-5}/(\pi\times200^2)=5.0\times10^{-11}$: 103.0 dB, or 103.1 dB with clear air.
- **Envelope at 200 m:** $0.1\times0.838\times1.55\times10^{-6}/(4\pi\times200)=5.2\times10^{-11}$ (102.9 dB), with $D_{\rm opt}=\sqrt{1.55\times10^{-6}\times200/\pi}/0.45=22$ mm.

**(b) The detection level.** Setting $\bar n=n_{\rm req}$ in the first equation gives the largest loss a point can tolerate,
$$\mathcal{L}_{\rm det}=\frac{n_{\rm req}\,E_{\rm ph}}{\eta\,E_{\rm tx}},$$
and the maximum range solves $\mathcal{L}(R_{\max})=\mathcal{L}_{\rm det}$. The level moves by 10 dB for every factor of 10 in $E_{\rm tx}$.

| Receiver | $\lambda$ | $n_{\rm req}$ | $\eta$ | $E_{\rm tx}$ per point | $\mathcal{L}_{\rm det}$ | Tolerable loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SPAD dToF, bright sun | 940 nm | 25 | 0.185 | 0.1 µJ (e.g. ≈10 shots of 10 nJ) | $2.9\times10^{-10}$ | 95.4 dB |
| Linear InGaAs APD ToF | 1,550 nm | ≈890 | 0.40 | 2 µJ (one fibre-laser pulse) | $1.4\times10^{-10}$ | 98.5 dB |
| FMCW | 1,550 nm | 30 | 0.25 | 0.3 µJ (30 mW × 10 µs) | $5.1\times10^{-11}$ | 102.9 dB |

*How the numbers are derived* [E]:
- **$n_{\rm req}$ and $\eta$** are the §C7a values, with the APD's photoelectron count taken from the amplifier-noise calculation of §C7b subsection 1(a):
  - SPAD: optics 0.5 × photon detection efficiency 0.37;
  - APD: optics 0.5 × quantum efficiency 0.8;
  - FMCW: the §C7a(a) efficiency.
- **Worked (SPAD):** $25\times2.11\times10^{-19}/(0.185\times10^{-7})=2.86\times10^{-10}$, and $-10\log_{10}(2.86\times10^{-10})=95.4$ dB.
- **Worked (FMCW):** $30\times1.28\times10^{-19}/(0.25\times3\times10^{-7})=5.13\times10^{-11}$, i.e. 102.9 dB.

**Reading Figure C0.10a** [E]:
- **Direct detection falls as $D^2/R^2$ at every range.** Setting $\rho D^2/(4R^2)=\mathcal{L}_{\rm det}$ gives $R_{\max}=\tfrac{D}{2}\sqrt{\rho/\mathcal{L}_{\rm det}}$.
  - SPAD case, $D=25$ mm: $0.0125\times\sqrt{0.1/2.86\times10^{-10}}=234$ m, or 229 m with clear air. The 10 and 50 mm lenses reach 93 and 450 m.
  - The APD case reaches 326 m with 25 mm. Its per-point need $n_{\rm req}E_{\rm ph}/\eta$ is 10× the SPAD's, but its 20× larger energy leaves a 2× margin, i.e. $\sqrt2\times$ the range.
- **Coherent detection is flat, then falls.** With 0.3 µJ, $D=10$ mm reaches 121 m and $D=25$ mm reaches 189 m.
  - The 50 mm aperture reaches *no* range: its near-field level, 107.0 dB, already exceeds the 102.9 dB budget.
  - At 1,310 nm the 25 mm aperture also fails, narrowly. Its near field is $10\log_{10}(1310/1550)^2=-1.5$ dB weaker, at 102.4 dB, and its 18% more energetic photons lower the budget to 102.2 dB.
- **Near the plateau, range is hypersensitive to the budget.**
  - For a fixed aperture, ignoring $T_{\rm atm}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\rm coh}(R)=\mathcal{L}_{\rm coh}(0)/[1+(R/z_R)^2]$, so $R_{\max}=z_R\sqrt{\mathcal{L}_{\rm coh}(0)/\mathcal{L}_{\rm det}-1}$.
  - For 25 mm: $257\times\sqrt{8.06/5.13-1}=194$ m, or 189 m with clear air.
  - Requiring $P_D=0.9$ with four speckles ($n_{\rm req}=45$, item (c)) raises the level by $10\log_{10}(45/30)=1.8$ dB and cuts the 25 mm range to **54 m**.
  - The best collimated aperture degrades gracefully instead, as $R_{\max}\propto1/n_{\rm req}$: from 198 m to 133 m.
- **At 200 m, the 20–25 mm apertures of §C7a are close to the optimum** ($D_{\rm opt}=22$ mm). A bigger collimated aperture helps only beyond its own Rayleigh range; at 500 m the optimum is 35 mm.

**(c) Where $n_{\rm req}$ comes from: detection statistics.** Each range bin is tested against a threshold set for a false-alarm probability $P_{FA}=10^{-6}$ per bin (subsection 7). The statistics differ by receiver.

*Photon counting (SPAD histogram).* A pixel that sums hundreds of speckles sees a non-fading signal, so signal and background counts are Poisson.
- **Rule:** with background mean $b$ per bin, the threshold is the smallest $k$ with $P(N\ge k\mid b)\le P_{FA}$, and
$$P_D=P(N\ge k\mid s+b)=\frac{\gamma(k,\,s+b)}{\Gamma(k)},$$
the regularized lower incomplete gamma function, which equals the Poisson tail. Here $s$ is the mean signal count.
- **Night, $b=0.01$:** $P(N\ge2\mid0.01)=5.0\times10^{-5}$ is too high and $P(N\ge3\mid0.01)=1.7\times10^{-7}$ passes, so $k=3$.
- **Bright sun, $b=2.2$** (the §C7a model's 4,430 background counts over ≈2,000 bins, §C7b subsection 1(a)): $P(N\ge12\mid2.2)=3.6\times10^{-6}$ fails and $P(N\ge13\mid2.2)=6.0\times10^{-7}$ passes, so $k=13$.

*Coherent detection (FMCW).* After the FFT, the power in one frequency bin is compared with a threshold.
- **Normalization:** the noise (LO shot noise) is set to unit mean, so noise-only power is exponential and the threshold is $x_{\rm th}=-\ln P_{FA}=13.8$. In a shot-noise-limited receiver the mean signal-to-noise ratio equals $\bar n$ (subsection 2).
- **Steady, non-fading target:** $P_D=Q_1\!\left(\sqrt{2\bar n},\sqrt{2x_{\rm th}}\right)$, where $Q_1$ is Marcum's Q function.
- **One speckle** (Rayleigh amplitude, exponential intensity; Swerling I): $P_D=P_{FA}^{1/(1+\bar n)}$.
- **$M$ independent speckles summed incoherently** (polarization, wavelength or spatial diversity), with $\bar n$ split equally among them:
  - the noise-only sum is Gamma-distributed, so the threshold $x_M$ solves $Q(M,x_M)=P_{FA}$, where $Q$ is the regularized upper incomplete gamma function;
  - then $P_D=Q\!\left(M,\,x_M/(1+\bar n/M)\right)$;
  - for $M=4$, $x_4=21.4$.

| Receiver statistics | $\bar n$ for $P_D=0.5$ | $\bar n$ for $P_D=0.9$ | $P_D$ at $\bar n=25$ | $P_D$ at $\bar n=30$ | $P_D$ at $\bar n=50$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Photon counting, night ($b=0.01$, $k=3$) | 2.7 | 5.3 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| Photon counting, bright sun ($b=2.2$, $k=13$) | 10.5 | 15.6 | 0.999 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| Coherent, steady target | 13.3 | 20.8 | 0.97 | 0.995 | 1.00 |
| Coherent, 4 speckles summed | 19.3 | 44.9 | 0.66 | 0.76 | 0.92 |
| Coherent, 1 speckle | 18.9 | 130 | 0.59 | 0.64 | 0.76 |

*How the numbers are derived* [E]:
- **Method:** each entry solves the $P_D$ expression above numerically (script).
- **Worked check, one speckle:**
  - at 90%, $1+\bar n=\ln P_{FA}/\ln P_D=(-13.82)/(-0.1054)=131$;
  - at $\bar n=30$, $P_D=(10^{-6})^{1/31}=e^{-13.82/31}=0.64$.

**Reading Figure C0.10b** [E]:
- **ToF:** the model's 25 counts are 1.6× the 15.6 that an ideal single-bin counter needs in bright sun. The margin covers a return spread over two or three bins, dead time and pile-up (§C6).
- **FMCW:** the model's 30 photoelectrons give $P_D\approx0.64$ with one speckle and 0.76 with four. A 90% requirement needs 45 with four speckles (close to Waymo's 50 [P]), or 130 with one. The §C7a energy per point is therefore, if anything, generous to FMCW.
- **Curve shapes:** photon counting has the steepest curves. Once the mean clears the threshold, a non-fading Poisson signal almost always crosses it. Speckle fading makes the coherent curves shallow, so the last few percent of $P_D$ cost the most photons.

**(d) How maximum range scales with energy per point and aperture.** Solve $\mathcal{L}(R_{\max})=\mathcal{L}_{\rm det}(E_{\rm tx})$, with $T_{\rm atm}\approx1$ in the closed forms.

- **Direct detection:**
$$R_{\max}=\frac{D}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\rho\,\eta\,E_{\rm tx}}{n_{\rm req}\,E_{\rm ph}}}\ \propto\sqrt{A_{\rm rx}E_{\rm tx}} .$$
  At fixed optical power $P_{\rm opt}$ the point rate is $\dot N=P_{\rm opt}/E_{\rm tx}$. So $\dot N R_{\max}^2=\rho\,\eta\,D^2P_{\rm opt}/(4n_{\rm req}E_{\rm ph})$ is constant: this is the range-weighted point-rate figure of merit of §C3b.
- **Coherent, fixed collimated aperture:** nothing is detected below
$$E_{\min}=\frac{n_{\rm req}E_{\rm ph}}{\eta\,\mathcal{L}_{\rm coh}(0)}=\frac{2\pi^2\,n_{\rm req}\,E_{\rm ph}\,w_0^2}{\eta\,\rho\,t^2\lambda^2}.$$
  - This is 0.031, 0.19 and 0.76 µJ for $D=10$, 25 and 50 mm.
  - Above it, $R_{\max}=z_R\sqrt{E_{\rm tx}/E_{\min}-1}$, which becomes the $\sqrt{E_{\rm tx}}$ law once $R_{\max}\gg z_R$.
  - Worked (25 mm): $2\pi^2\times30\times1.28\times10^{-19}\times0.01125^2/(0.25\times0.1\times0.838\times(1.55\times10^{-6})^2)=9.6\times10^{-21}/5.0\times10^{-14}=1.9\times10^{-7}$ J.
- **Coherent, best collimated aperture at each range:**
$$R_{\max}=\frac{\rho\,t^2\lambda\,\eta\,E_{\rm tx}}{4\pi\,n_{\rm req}E_{\rm ph}}\ \propto E_{\rm tx},\qquad \dot N\,R_{\max}=\text{const}.$$
  - Worked: $0.1\times0.838\times1.55\times10^{-6}\times0.25\times3\times10^{-7}/(4\pi\times30\times1.28\times10^{-19})=9.7\times10^{-15}/4.8\times10^{-17}=202$ m (198 m with clear air).
  - Doubling the energy doubles the range, but only if the aperture grows as $\sqrt R$, while the footprint's share of a fixed angular cell shrinks as $1/R$ (§C10.3a).
- **Equal apertures, constant gap.** At $D=D_{\rm opt}(R)$, the ratio of the energies per sample needed by FMCW and SPAD ToF does not depend on range:
$$\frac{E_{\rm FMCW}}{E_{\rm SPAD}}=\frac{n_{\rm c}E_{\rm ph,c}/\eta_{\rm c}}{n_{\rm d}E_{\rm ph,d}/\eta_{\rm d}}\cdot\frac{A_{\rm rx}}{A_{\rm mode}}=0.54\times\frac{1}{t^2(0.45)^2}=0.54\times5.9=3.2 .$$
  - The subscripts c and d mark the coherent and direct-detection cases.
  - The first factor is $(30\times1.28\times10^{-19}/0.25)/(25\times2.11\times10^{-19}/0.185)=1.54\times10^{-17}/2.86\times10^{-17}=0.54$.
  - With clear-air loss the ratio is 3.1 at 100–500 m.
- **Against a fixed 25 mm SPAD receiver**, the gap per sample closes as $1/R$, because the optimal coherent aperture grows: 8.1× at 100 m, 4.0× at 200 m, 2.6× at 300 m and 1.6× at 500 m.
- **Linear APD against SPAD with the same lens:** $(890\times1.28\times10^{-19}/0.40)/(25\times2.11\times10^{-19}/0.185)=10.0$. That is ten times the energy per point at every range, which the 1,550 nm eye-safety limits can pay for at low point rates (§C4c).

**(e) What these figures leave out.**
- **Small objects.** An object smaller than the footprint intercepts a fraction of the light that falls as $1/R^2$, so its loss grows as $R^{-4}$ (subsection 1; §E12). A narrow coherent beam is filled by smaller objects, but it must hit them (§C10.3a).
- **Background-limited direct detection.** When sunlight dominates, range scales as $A_{\rm rx}^{1/4}$ rather than $A_{\rm rx}^{1/2}$ (subsection 1).
- **Eye safety** caps $E_{\rm tx}$ per direction and per unit time (subsection 5, §C4c). It would appear as a wavelength-dependent vertical limit in Figure C0.10c.
- **Focusing.** Focusing a coherent beam at a set range $R_f$ gives the far-field level $\rho t^2w_0^2/(2R_f^2)$ there, restoring $D^2$ scaling at that range only.
- **Turbulence, misalignment and non-Lambertian targets** lower $\mathcal{L}_{\rm coh}$ further (Frehlich and Kavaya 1991). Weather multiplies both losses by $e^{-2\alpha R}$ (subsection 8).
